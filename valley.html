The Valley

A place of ancient generosity · Un lugar de antigua generosidad

First People / Los Primeros Pueblos

The Generous People

For thousands of years before European contact, the Puyallup Valley was home to the spuyaləpabš — the Puyallup people — whose name means "people from the bend at the bottom of the river." The neighboring Yakama Tribe called them the "pough-allup" — the generous people — a name that reflected both their welcoming spirit and the extraordinary abundance of their homeland.

They lived in permanent cedar longhouses along the banks of the Puyallup River, sustained by salmon runs so thick the river seemed alive, by camas roots and wapato, by berries, game, and the deep forests of fir, cedar, and alder that once covered this valley. Tahoma — what settlers would call Mt. Rainier — watched over everything, its glaciers feeding the rivers, its ancient eruptions creating the rich volcanic soil that makes this one of the most fertile valleys on earth.

In 1854, the Medicine Creek Treaty — signed under deeply problematic circumstances — displaced the Puyallup people from most of their ancestral lands. The Puyallup Tribe has fought ever since to reclaim their rights, winning landmark victories including the 1974 Boldt Decision restoring tribal fishing rights and a historic 1990 Land Claims Settlement. Today the Puyallup Tribe of Indians is a sovereign nation and a major force in Pierce County.

The Land / La Tierra

Volcanic Soil, Glacier Water

The Puyallup Valley was shaped by geology as much as by human hands. Around 370 B.C., a massive eruption from Mt. Rainier sent mudflows down the Carbon and Puyallup Rivers, depositing the rich alluvial soil that defines the valley floor. Glacial meltwater from Tahoma continues to feed the rivers and nourish the ground, making this one of the most productive growing regions in the Pacific Northwest.

European settlers arrived in the 1850s and quickly recognized what the Puyallup people had always known — this land was extraordinarily generous. By 1865, hops farming had taken hold and the valley became one of the major hop-growing regions in the country. When hop lice ended that era in 1892, farmers turned to berries — blackberries, raspberries, strawberries — and by 1912 the Puyallup and Sumner Fruitgrowers' Association had 1,300 members, considered the largest fruit growers association in the world.

The Washington State Fair — first held in 1900 as the "Valley Fair" — grew from this agricultural tradition and remains one of the largest state fairs in the country. The annual Daffodil Festival, the bulb farms, the WSU Research and Extension Center — all of it rooted in this valley's remarkable fertility.

Today / Hoy

Growing Forward

Over 70% of Pierce County's farmland has been lost since 1950 to housing developments and commercial growth. Organizations like PCC Farmland Trust are working to conserve what remains — because this land is irreplaceable.

We grow here with full awareness of that history — the generosity of the Puyallup people, the fertility of volcanic soil, the work of generations of farmers, and the urgent need to keep growing food on land that was made for it. Every tomato planted is a small act of continuity.
